# Bladefall — copy-ready music prompts

Requested by U116–U117. Creative recommendations, not assertions about any ElevenLabs feature or a guarantee of generated loop quality. Use these alongside SOUNDTRACK_PLAN_2026-09-20.md. Existing tracks have not been auditioned, so use selected existing songs as audible references when deciding whether a new result belongs in the same score. Keep the original files.

## Shared musical direction

Aerth is a dark-fantasy world with an ultimately hopeful story. The score should feel human and adventurous: expressive strings, selective woodwinds, hand-played percussion, weathered brass, and unusual textures for magic. Distinguish regions through instrumentation and rhythm, not only faster/slower versions of one battle theme. Avoid constant trailer-style impacts and giant climaxes in exploration loops. Leave room for footsteps, danger warnings and spoken dialogue. These first-pass prompts are instrumental, without sung lyrics, spoken voices or recognizable borrowed melodies. That is a recommended starting point, not a restriction on later deliberate vocal choices.

Treat suggested tempo/duration as creative targets. For exploration, ask for a steady middle section that can loop and an ending that returns naturally to the opening harmony. An audio editor may still need to make the actual loop seamless. For cinematic cues, use sections tied to story events rather than assuming dialogue or player actions finish at fixed times.

Create ONE simple original home melody for Briar. Reuse that actual melody in the menu, hub and Ian/ending arrangements after choosing it; asking an unrelated generation for a similar mood alone will not ensure the same melody. Likewise choose and retain a coastal melody for the beach/cliffs/crossing and a Sunspire melody for courtyard/library. Supply the chosen reference where the creation workflow permits, or arrange from it afterward. No implied requirement to generate all tracks in one request.

## Five missing main-level themes

### 1. Home Worth Saving — Briar Town, part one

Target: 3–4 minutes; roughly 80–95 BPM. Warm, vulnerable, determined.

> Instrumental dark-fantasy game exploration music for a small farming village preparing to defend itself. Begin with an intimate, memorable melody on fiddle and wooden flute, supported by plucked strings and soft low strings. Add restrained hand drums and a faint sense of danger beneath the warmth. This is an ordinary person's home: family, muddy fields, a working mill and frightened neighbors helping each other. Courage is just beginning; do not make it sound like the hero has already won. Keep space for conversation and sound effects. Develop gently without a huge climax. Return naturally to the opening harmony for a repeatable exploration loop. No lyrics or spoken voices.

Also request a lighter version of the chosen result for Thomas/Mara conversations and title/character creation. The defensive encounters can introduce drums without replacing its melody.

### 2. Shipwreck Shore — Storm Coast, part one

Target: 3–4 minutes; roughly 85–105 BPM. Resourceful, curious, weather-beaten.

> Instrumental fantasy coastal exploration music for a shore full of broken ships, hidden cargo and survivors preparing a boat. Use plucked strings, low fiddle, occasional wooden flute and light frame drums with an uneven nautical sway. Suggest salt wind, creaking timber and an approaching storm through musical texture rather than literal weather recordings. The mood is adventurous and scrappy, with danger beyond the horizon. The player is searching, repairing and solving problems, not celebrating in a tavern. Avoid a comic pirate jig or a nonstop sea shanty. Introduce a clear original coastal melody that can return later during the voyage. Keep the arrangement spacious and loop-friendly. No lyrics or voices.

### 3. Thunder Cliffs — Storm Coast, part two

Target: 3–4 minutes; roughly 100–115 BPM. Exposed height, effort, discovery.

> Instrumental fantasy exploration and climbing music for towering sea cliffs on a remote island. Build from the established Storm Coast melody, now carried by rising strings and breathy woodwinds, with low drums that pulse like distant thunder. Alternate broad, spacious phrases with short urgent passages for narrow ledges and wind-swept crossings. The player can see the sea far below and a beautiful palace high above. Convey danger and wonder together. This is sustained traversal and discovery, not a boss fight; avoid relentless pounding or a huge final resolution. Use a darker undertone beneath bright high notes and return to a stable loop. No lyrics or spoken voices.

### 4. Palace Above the Clouds — Sunspire Palace, part one

Target: 3–4 minutes; roughly 70–90 BPM. Beauty under occupation.

> Instrumental fantasy exploration score for an immense white-marble palace above the clouds, once a home of sacred knowledge and now occupied by an invading army. Use harp, delicate bells, warm strings, distant horns and restrained low percussion. Let the main melody feel beautiful, wise and welcoming, while lower notes suggest that the peace has been disturbed. The player crosses hanging gardens, sunlit courts and guarded balconies, helping survivors reclaim the palace. Avoid a church service, a battle march or a victory finale. Preserve quiet space for dialogue, puzzles and environmental sounds. Write an original Sunspire melody suitable for a quieter library arrangement later. Gently developing, repeatable ending. No vocals.

### 5. The Sky Library — Sunspire Palace, part two

Target: 3–4 minutes; roughly 60–80 BPM or a gentle free pulse. Wonder, secrecy, discovery.

> Instrumental fantasy library exploration music using a quiet variation of the established Sunspire melody. Picture enormous shelves, ancient books, floating dust in golden light and walkways turning around a vast central hall. Use soft harp patterns, high string harmonics, a low warm string bed and occasional clear bell notes. Create wonder and the feeling that important knowledge is close, with mild tension from the occupying guards. Avoid horror, mournful grief and heavy drums. Leave generous space for reading and conversation. The music should invite careful exploration and never announce the answer to a mystery. Slow, gently changing phrases with a natural looping passage. No singing or spoken voices.

Also request a sparse question-room version: fewer instruments, long pauses and subtle magical depth for the orb. Its later soul vision can borrow a brief unsettling texture from the Void cue.

## Two missing boss themes and the ship crossing

### 6. The Fallen — Ruined Keep boss

Target: 2–3 minutes, loopable; roughly 115–135 BPM. A skilled, dangerous opponent.

> Instrumental dark-fantasy boss music for a close duel with a disciplined Hollowed champion in a ruined indoor hall. Tight low strings, short sharp string answers, measured war drums and restrained brass. The fighter was once a person; include a quiet trace of sorrow beneath the threat. Emphasize tension, timing and sudden openings rather than the huge stomping rhythm of a giant monster. Build an intense but controlled loop, with a stronger variation for the final part of the duel. Leave room for attack warning sounds and weapon impacts. No long peaceful introduction, no comic melody, no vocals and no large victory ending inside the fight loop.

### 7. Bound by Chains — Storm Coast hydra

Target: about 3 minutes, loopable; roughly 95–115 BPM with heavy accents. Fear mixed with pity.

> Instrumental fantasy boss music for an enormous multi-headed sea creature chained against storm cliffs. Use deep drums, surging low strings, strained metallic percussion and powerful dark brass, with a mournful melody suggesting pain beneath its anger. The creature is trapped and defending itself; the player breaks its chains to free it. Make the scale frightening without portraying it as an evil tyrant. Build rolling waves of pressure with clear breathing spaces for attack tells. Keep the battle section loopable without resolving. Separately create a short gentle release ending that turns the mournful melody toward relief as the last chain breaks. No vocals or generic triumphant monster-kill finale.

### 8. Across the Storm — ship-crossing minigame

Target: 2–3 minutes, loopable; roughly 125–145 BPM. Momentum and teamwork.

> Instrumental fantasy sailing action music built around the Storm Coast melody. A small repaired boat crosses dangerous water toward a distant island while its crew steers around wreckage and fights attackers. Use quick strings, driving frame drums, bold but brief horn phrases and bright accents that suggest spray and sudden turns. Keep an easy-to-follow forward pulse with rising and falling waves of intensity. It should feel exciting and risky, not hopeless or like the final battle of the game. Avoid long breaks that would drain the pace. Create a repeatable central action loop and a separate short safe-arrival ending. No lyrics or spoken voices.

## Hub and rifts

### 9. A Place to Return — Waystation hub

Target: 3–5 minutes; roughly 60–80 BPM. Safety without forgetting the outside world.

> Instrumental fantasy refuge music, warm and restful enough for long visits. Use gentle strings, a wooden flute, soft plucked instruments and very little percussion. Quote the chosen Briar home melody in a calmer arrangement. People here mend armor, care for animals, share news and prepare to help each other; they are tired but not defeated. Include a small shadow of sadness, balanced by growing hope. Avoid tavern comedy, busy solo passages, huge swells or repeating dramatic impacts. The music must sit comfortably under shop menus and spoken conversations. Create a soft, natural loop with no abrupt ending. No vocals or environmental recordings.

### 10. Lessons Beyond the Rift — Rift Hall and class mentors

Target: 3 minutes; calm version roughly 65–85 BPM, training variation roughly 105–120 BPM. Safe magic and preserved knowledge.

> Instrumental fantasy music for a quiet hall of violet crystalline portals that preserve fighting disciplines. Use clear glass-like chimes, gentle strings and a subtle pulsing pattern. The portals lead to mentors and trials of skill; this place is mysterious but welcoming, not a prison or a source of evil. Give it a memorable short melody associated with discovery and learning. Keep the hall version calm and spacious under dialogue. Create a second version of the same melody with controlled drums and quicker strings for training challenges, while preserving its welcoming identity. Both versions should loop naturally. No ominous horror choir, lyrics or spoken voices.

## The ending suite: three separate prompts from one shared musical idea

These are three game cues, not one long track that assumes fixed dialogue length. This is composition #11 in the placement plan.

### 11A. Beyond the Gate — Void arrival

Target: 60–120 seconds with an extendable quiet middle. Shock, scale, trapped lives.

> Instrumental dark-fantasy cinematic ambience immediately after a tyrant's defeat opens an overwhelming breach and pulls the hero into a vast black Void. Start with a brief collapsing swell, then leave room for near-silence. Use deep restrained string tones, distant glass-like notes and slow unstable harmonies to suggest countless trapped souls and impossible distance. No triumphant victory theme: the hero has won the fight but has not yet saved these people. Avoid loud repetitive hits or whispered words that could clash with dialogue. End on an unresolved, quiet texture that can continue until the next scene begins. No vocals.

### 11B. Ian's Promise — meeting Ian's spirit

Target: 90–150 seconds with a low-intensity dialogue loop. Earned recognition, warmth, legacy.

> Instrumental fantasy cinematic music for a young hero meeting the spirit of the legendary protector who came before them. Begin very simply: a warm solo cello or viola, a few soft harp notes, then gentle supporting strings. Carefully introduce the chosen Briar home melody, now slower and more assured. The moment is intimate and sincere: recognition, an unexpected truth about the hero's childhood, and responsibility passed from one generation to the next. Hope enters the darkness without becoming a grand victory yet. Leave ample space for the actors' voices. Sustain a quiet middle section for variable dialogue length. No sung or spoken vocals.

### 11C. Cut the Binding — final charge

Target: short entrance, a 30–60 second repeatable rising-pressure section, separate success resolution. Desperate effort becoming hope.

> Instrumental fantasy climax for the hero charging a legendary blade to break a prison holding countless souls. Begin with the warm hero melody from the preceding scene, then add urgent low strings, rising brass and a clear accelerating feeling through denser rhythms rather than constant tempo changes. The hero struggles against an immense force as time runs out. Make the repeated action feel powerful and purposeful, not like a comic button-mashing game. Provide a sustained high-pressure loop that can continue until success. Create a separate decisive musical strike and bright release for the successful cut, leaving room to transition into a peaceful restoration track. No vocals and no victory before the success cue.

For a failed charge, make a very short falling unresolved sting. Retry only the charge cue; do not replay Ian's speech, the boss music or a full death theme. Five consecutive failures offer the already-approved skip into the successful resolution.
